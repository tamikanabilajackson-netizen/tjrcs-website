Every form I've filled out in fifteen years of this work has a box for challenges.

Behaviours to monitor. Goals to work toward. Support needs. Areas of concern. Whoever designed these forms clearly thought hard about what a person struggles with, because there's always room for it. Sometimes a full page.

For a long time, there was no box for what a person was good at.

Not missing on purpose. Just never built in, the way a house built without a certain window means nobody thought to ask what the room would look like with more light.

I noticed it slowly, filling out the same kind of form for different people over different years, in different buildings, for different reasons. I'd write pages under "challenges" without pausing. Then I'd get to the bottom of the form and realize there was nowhere left to write that this same person could make anyone in the room laugh, or noticed things nobody else caught, or had opinions about music that could talk your ear off for an hour.

That information didn't disappear. It just had nowhere official to go.

I think about that a lot when people ask why Build & Launch starts the way it does.

Most programs, even good ones, are built around the boxes that already exist. They start with what needs fixing because that's the form they were handed. It's not always a lack of care. It's often just inherited structure, the shape of a form nobody questioned.

I wanted to build the box first, before anything else.

Session one of Build & Launch isn't an assessment of what's wrong. It's an actual conversation about what a young adult is already good at, what they notice, what they'd talk about for an hour if someone let them. That page goes in their journal before anything about challenges does, because I wanted the order to mean something.

It's not that challenges don't exist or don't matter. They do, and we get to them. But I've watched what happens when a person hears "tell me what you're good at" before they hear anything else, and it changes the whole conversation that follows. People sit up straighter. They stop bracing.

Strengths-based isn't a tone or a slogan. It's an order of operations. It's deciding, on purpose, which question gets asked first, because the form always gets filled out eventually, and whichever box comes first tends to become the whole story.

I didn't want anyone's story to start with the empty box.
